Teaching a CNC to Touch

A probe that learns the shape of stones, shells and gears by touch, round by round. Unfinished on purpose.

I want to engrave stones: pebbles and shells as they come out of the sea, round and tilted, every one different. A small desktop CNC can cut a fine line, but only if it knows exactly where the surface is. A camera cannot tell the height of a stone to a hundredth of a millimetre. A finger can.

So the first part of this project is teaching the machine to feel. A thin steel tip moves towards the object until it touches it, records where, lifts and tries again somewhere else, thousands of times, like someone reading a face with their fingertips. It is also where the work meets an older idea of mine, Imagine being blind: what does an object look like when all you have is touch?

Vibe coding

I did not write the software line by line. I described what I wanted, watched the machine, and argued with an AI about what went wrong. The AI wrote and rewrote the scripts and built a simulator to test them before they touched metal; many of the ideas, and all the broken probe tips, came from me watching the machine. That back and forth between a person, a machine and an AI is the method, and the reason for this new category.

The Fourier idea

The breakthrough came from a dream. Instead of crawling along the edge, circle the object from far away: sixteen touches from outside give a rough outline. Then, between every two neighbours, predict where the edge should be and touch there, again from outside. Then again at half the spacing, until the outline stops changing: coarse to fine, like adding the higher terms of a Fourier series. Every touch comes from outside, so the tip never has to find its way out of a gap.

Two bent probes later, the first law of the project is: always escape up. When something is wrong, the tool never moves sideways; it lifts straight up.

Progress log 6

  1. 22 November 2026

    The gear, mapped overnight

    I left the machine alone with the gear for a night and a morning. It finished the outline from the side, then mapped the whole surface by touching down from above: 290 outline points, about 3,950 heights, and the hole in the middle marked as empty air. 5,937 measurements in all.

    Around midday on Sunday it stopped and reported that the object had moved. Nothing visible had moved, and the probe was still conducting, so the next step is a way to find the origin again from a few outline points and heights. Then the gear can finally be engraved.

  2. 21 November 2026

    Skip forward, and a second sense

    A scan can now stop refining the outline when it has enough, map the height, and finish the open edges by looking from above only, which is far safer for the probe.

    Designed version 5: the object plate rests on four thin pressure sensors. A touch is now electrical contact OR a jump in pressure OR pressure changing fast, so the machine also notices what its tip cannot feel. If the little guard board fails, the machine stops. Not built yet.

  3. 20 November 2026

    Limits, and looking from above

    Every sideways touch may now only go 1-2 mm past where the edge is expected; if nothing is felt, the tool lifts and comes down from above to look. My idea while watching points being missed by a hair: keep going, land, and halve the distance from above until the edge is found.

    The script also learned to run without asking: skip a doubtful spot, come back to it in a later round with a small change.

  4. 19 November 2026

    The Fourier outline, and a second bent probe

    The coarse-to-fine outline works in the simulator on a pebble, a shell, a notched shape and the gear, with pause and resume even after the power was cut. On the real gear the first run bent another tip: contact was felt 7 mm too late. Since then nothing on the tool may hang lower than the object, and the software distrusts itself.

  5. 18 November 2026

    The gear, and the first crash

    A gear is the worst case: forty teeth, steep walls, gaps narrower than the tip. The plate moved, a bit broke, and one rule too many drove the probe into the root between two teeth. Lesson: always escape up.

  6. 17 November 2026

    First scan: a broken caliper slider

    Walking around the object along its edge, then a height map that gets denser where the surface bends: 169 outline points and 2,753 heights.
